# When is a grid worth running, and when is it just paying the exchange?

Generated by `python run_backtest.py --all`. Every figure is measured on real CoinW BTC_USDT data, not asserted.

A grid strategy is usually sold as if it were free money. It is not: it is a specific trade -- you give up trend participation in exchange for harvesting oscillation, and you pay the exchange on every leg. This document is the honest map of when that trade is good.

---

## 1. Grid versus simply holding, in both numeraires

The second half of this table is the one most comparisons omit. If you are a BTC holder, your question is not "did I make USDT" but **"did I end up with more BTC"** -- which is exactly what a coin-margined (幣本位) grid is meant to deliver.

| Market | BTC moved | Grid (USDT) | Hold (USDT) | Winner | Grid ends with | Hold ends with | Winner |
|---|---:|---:|---:|:--|---:|---:|:--|
| `bear-2022` | -64.2% | -33.36% | -64.25% | **grid** | 0.4030 BTC | 0.2162 BTC | **grid** |
| `range-2023` | +155.6% | +12.32% | +155.39% | hold | 0.2657 BTC | 0.6041 BTC | hold |
| `bull-2020Q4` | +445.1% | +10.82% | +444.53% | hold | 0.1887 BTC | 0.9270 BTC | hold |
| `flat-2024-26` | -2.6% | -5.81% | -2.71% | hold | 0.1496 BTC | 0.1546 BTC | hold |
| `year-2025` | -6.4% | -0.55% | -6.45% | **grid** | 0.1135 BTC | 0.1067 BTC | **grid** |
| `chop-2019H2` | -21.5% | +11.55% | -21.55% | **grid** | 1.3088 BTC | 0.9204 BTC | **grid** |
| `ytd-2026` | -28.2% | -11.22% | -28.25% | **grid** | 0.1410 BTC | 0.1140 BTC | **grid** |
| `full-2019-2026` | +1600.6% | +56.13% | +1598.88% | hold | 0.2481 BTC | 2.6991 BTC | hold |

**The pattern is unambiguous and it holds in both numeraires.** The grid wins when the market falls or chops sideways, and loses when it trends up. In the 2022 bear it finished with substantially *more BTC* than simply holding -- which is the coin-margined pitch, and it is real. In the bull windows it finished with a fraction of the BTC, which is the same mechanism running in reverse.

**So the honest advice is: if you have a directional view that the market goes up, buy spot and hold. A grid is not a better way to be long.** A grid is what you run when you think the market goes sideways or down and you want to be paid for the chop.

## 1b. Against what a retail trader would actually run instead

Holding is not the only alternative. The two strategies most likely to be running in a drawdown are **periodic DCA** (定投) and a **DCA-martingale bot** (馬丁) of the kind Pionex, 3Commas and OKX all ship. Both are simulated on the same bars with the same fees, and because these bots execute with market orders, both pay the **taker** fee plus adverse slippage. The grid pays maker on most fills -- that is a real structural advantage of resting orders, not a modelling favour.

Martingale parameters are the conventional retail defaults, not tuned: base order 2% of capital, up to 8 safety orders each 1.5x the last, triggered every 2.5% below the previous entry, take-profit 1.5% on the cycle's average cost.

| Martingale (DCA bot) | **-32.24%** | 42.62% | **0.4097 BTC** |

**The martingale rows are the ones to read carefully.** Its ladder is finite. Once all eight safety orders are spent the bot is simply fully exposed, waiting for price to return to average cost, and the note under each table reports exactly how much of the window it spent in that state. Martingale converts a drawdown into a *frozen* position, which is why its drawdown is consistently the worst of the three active strategies.

**DCA is the honest long-run competitor.** Over the full 2019-2026 run it beats this grid comfortably, because deploying gradually into a rising market is a good idea and selling into that same rise is not. In the falling and choppy windows the ordering reverses.

## 2. Does adding more grid levels help? No -- it pays the exchange.

The most common retail intuition is that a denser grid catches more moves and therefore earns more. Measured on the two-year `flat-2024-26` window, holding the covered range roughly constant and varying only the number of levels:

| Levels | Spacing | Round trips | Fee drag | Return | Max DD |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 6 **(default)** | 3.45% | 117 | **6.0%** | -5.81% | 29.70% |
| 12 | 1.73% | 233 | **12.1%** | -9.16% | 25.34% |
| 25 | 0.83% | 422 | **25.7%** | -13.07% | 20.28% |
| 50 | 0.41% | 600 | **48.4%** | -9.66% | 12.14% |
| 100 | 0.35% | 620 | **57.8%** | -5.24% | 6.29% |

Round trips rise with density exactly as the intuition predicts. So does fee drag -- from single digits to well over half of gross spread capture. **At 100 levels the exchange takes more of the gross profit than the trader does.** Drawdown falls, because each level is smaller, so a dense grid is a lower-risk way to make no money.

### Ruling out our own fill rule as the cause

This engine normally allows one fill per level per UTC day (rule F7), which is stricter than a real bot and could by itself cap a dense ladder. A conclusion that dense grids are bad has to survive removing that cap, so here it is removed -- levels re-arm on the next bar:

| Levels | Round trips (F7 on) | Return (F7 on) | Round trips (F7 off) | Return (F7 off) |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 6 | 117 | -5.81% | 141 | -5.06% |
| 12 | 233 | -9.16% | 252 | -17.39% |
| 25 | 422 | -13.07% | 421 | -23.84% |
| 50 | 600 | -9.66% | 620 | -14.82% |
| 100 | 620 | -5.24% | 661 | -7.22% |

Relaxing the cap gives dense grids *more* fills and makes them **worse**, because the extra fills do not cover their own fees. The finding is a property of the cost structure, not of our fill model.

## 3. What we deliberately did NOT test, and why

Several grid variants sold in the market are missing from this study. That is a choice, not an oversight, and the reason matters more than a number would.

| Variant | Status | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Long spot grid** (做多網格) | Tested | This Skill. Everything above. |
| **Coin-margined view** (幣本位網格) | Tested | Section 1's right-hand columns. Same engine, different numeraire. |
| **Grid + base position** (中性/偏多配置) | Tested | `results/sensitivity.md` section 2b. It is a linear blend toward buy-and-hold, not a separate strategy. |
| **Infinity grid** (天地網格) | Partially | An infinity grid is a base position plus an upper-unbounded ladder. Section 2b covers the base half; the unbounded half changes nothing in a falling market, which is where the grid earns. |
| **Short grid** (做空網格) | **Not tested** | Requires perpetual futures. On CoinW's BTC perp the funding rate has historically been positive more often than not, meaning shorts are *paid* to hold. Simulating a short grid without real funding data would understate its return by an unknown but systematically-signed amount. A wrong number with a known bias is worse than no number. |
| **Leveraged / futures grid** (合約網格) | **Not tested** | Same funding problem, plus liquidation. A leveraged grid's entire risk profile is dominated by the liquidation price, and modelling that without the exchange's real margin tiers, mark-price rules and ADL behaviour would produce a backtest that is confident and wrong. |

**What it would take to add them honestly:** the historical funding-rate series for the instrument, the margin-tier table, and the mark-price methodology -- then the same fill model, plus a liquidation check on every bar. That is a second study, not a parameter.

**The intuition worth stating anyway,** clearly labelled as reasoning rather than measurement: in a fast directional move, a leveraged directional position captures the move while a grid is busy selling into it. Everything measured above is consistent with that. If you are confident about direction, a grid is the wrong instrument -- it is designed for the case where you are *not*.

---

## 4. The one-paragraph answer

Run a grid when you expect a range or a decline, when you want to be paid for volatility without taking a directional view, and when your fee tier leaves room above `2f/(1-f)` = 0.2002% per round trip. Do not run one because it looks like free money, do not add levels hoping for more profit, and do not run one at all if you believe the market is going up -- in that case buy spot and hold, which beat this strategy in every trending window tested, in both USDT and BTC terms.
